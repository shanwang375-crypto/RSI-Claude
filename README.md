# RSI-Claude
The Relative Strength Index (RSI) is a momentum oscillator used in technical analysis that measures the speed and magnitude of directional price movements. It was developed by J. Welles Wilder Jr. and introduced in his 1978 book *New Concepts in Technical Trading Systems*. The RSI fluctuates between 0 and 100 and is primarily used to identify overbought or oversold conditions in an asset.

### Calculation and Interpretation

The RSI is calculated using the average gains and losses over a specified period, traditionally 14 trading days. The formula is:

**RSI = 100 – [100 / (1 + (Average Gain / Average Loss))]**

In Wilder's original model, an RSI reading above 70 conventionally signals that an asset is **overbought** (potentially overvalued and due for a pullback), while a reading below 30 indicates it is **oversold** (potentially undervalued and due for a rebound). Some sources also note that a cross above or below the 50 level can signal a shift in trend momentum.

### Key Uses and Limitations

Beyond absolute levels, analysts use RSI **divergence**—when price makes a new high but the RSI fails to do so (bearish divergence), or vice versa (bullish divergence)—as a warning of potential trend reversals. However, the RSI is a lagging indicator based solely on past price data and does not account for fundamental factors or news events. Therefore, it is generally recommended for use alongside other analytical tools rather than in isolation.
